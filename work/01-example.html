An overdue upgrade

The problem

Four years behind the release.

Nothing could be patched.

Everything still had to ship.

01 — What was there

A site nobody could safely touch

Wagtail 2.x on a Python the platform had stopped shipping. Eleven packages pinned to versions with no upgrade path, a template layer that had been forked rather than extended, and a test suite that had been red long enough that nobody read it any more.

The practical consequence was not aesthetic. Every security advisory arrived with no way to act on it, and every new feature request was quoted at three times what it should have cost because the ground moved when anyone stood on it.

02 — Where it started

Read before writing

The first two weeks produced no visible change. I mapped which page models were actually in use, which templates were reachable, and which of those eleven pins were load-bearing rather than historical. Four of them turned out to be unused imports.

That map is the deliverable people forget to ask for. It decided the order of everything that followed, and it is the reason the upgrade could be quoted at a fixed price instead of hourly.

03 — The upgrade

One version at a time, in public

Wagtail's own upgrade path is version by version for a reason, and skipping releases to save time is how a two-week job becomes a two-month one. Each step got its own branch, its own deploy to a staging copy with production data, and its own sign-off.

Editorial work never stopped. The team kept publishing on the live site throughout, and each staging round was rebuilt from a fresh database copy rather than asking anyone to pause.

04 — What was left behind

Documented, so I am replaceable

The forked templates went back to extending the parent. The test suite runs green and runs in CI. The upgrade notes live in the repository, not in my head, with the reasoning for each decision that was not obvious.

The measure of this kind of work is whether the next person needs me. They should not. That is what makes it worth paying for.
