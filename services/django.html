and want the first six months of decisions made by someone who has watched a few of them go wrong. Most of what makes an application painful later is settled in the first fortnight.

What the work actually is

  • 01
    Data modelling

    Models that match what the business actually does, with the migration path thought through. This is the part that is cheap now and expensive in a year.

  • 02
    Application features

    Views, forms, permissions, background jobs, admin. The unglamorous middle of an application, done so the next person can read it.

  • 03
    REST APIs

    Django REST Framework, versioned, documented, and authenticated properly — whether the consumer is your own frontend or someone else's system.

  • 04
    Query and performance work

    Finding the N+1s, adding the indexes that matter, and caching only where measurement says to. Numbers before and after, not adjectives.

  • 05
    Tests that get run

    Enough coverage on the paths that would cost money if they broke, running in CI. A suite nobody trusts is worse than none — it teaches the team to ignore red.

What it costs

Scope decides it, and on an existing codebase the state of that codebase decides it as much as the feature list does. A well-tested application takes changes cheaply; one without tests charges you for the caution.

Defined pieces of work are quoted fixed. Anything open-ended starts with a short paid look at the code, producing a written plan and a fixed number for the rest — the plan is yours either way.

Questions I get asked

Do we need a rewrite?
Almost never, and I will usually argue against it. A rewrite spends the whole budget getting back to where you already are. What normally helps is fixing the two or three places the model has drifted from the business, which is a fraction of the cost.
Can you work with our existing team?
Yes, and often that is the arrangement — I take the piece nobody has the time or the specific experience for, in your repository, following your conventions rather than importing mine.
What about Python versions?
Old Pythons and old Djangos are routine. The upgrade is usually a known quantity; what makes it slow is dependencies pinned to versions that were never going to move, and that is worth finding out early.
